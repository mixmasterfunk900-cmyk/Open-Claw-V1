# Source Notes — The Experiment Where Ordinary People Obeyed a Stranger

## Working thesis
Milgram's obedience studies are not evidence that ordinary people are mindless machines. The careful version is darker: many ordinary people can be persuaded to continue harming someone when harm is framed as duty, science, progress, or someone else's responsibility. The video should preserve the famous 65% result while making room for later criticism: belief varied, procedures were imperfect, and modern scholars dispute the simple “blind obedience” reading.

## Core historical facts to use
- Stanley Milgram, a social psychologist at Yale University, began the obedience studies in New Haven in 1961, shortly after the Adolf Eichmann trial brought “following orders” into public view.
- Participants were recruited as men from the New Haven area for a supposed study of memory and learning. They were paid $4.50.
- In the baseline/remote condition reported in Milgram's 1963 paper, participants were assigned the role of “teacher”; the “learner” was a confederate/actor.
- The teacher sat before a shock generator marked from 15 to 450 volts, in 15-volt increments, ending with labels including “Danger: Severe Shock” and “XXX.”
- The learner's protests were staged. Participants believed, or were led to believe, that the shocks could be real.
- The experimenter used a sequence of verbal prods, including “Please continue,” “The experiment requires that you continue,” “It is absolutely essential that you continue,” and “You have no other choice; you must go on.”
- In Milgram's first published condition, 26 of 40 participants — 65% — went to the maximum 450 volts. All 40 went to at least 300 volts.
- Many participants showed strain: sweating, trembling, nervous laughter, protest, argument, or visible conflict.
- Ethical issues: deception, distress, disputed adequacy/timing of debriefing, and lack of fully informed consent by modern standards.

## Sources

### Stanley Milgram — original study
- Stanley Milgram, “Behavioral Study of Obedience,” *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*, 67(4), 371–378, 1963.
- DOI commonly listed: 10.1037/h0040525.
- Key use: primary source for procedure, shock generator, 40-participant sample, 65% maximum-shock result, prods, participant tension, and Milgram's interpretation of obedience.
- Caveat: use as Milgram's published account, not the final word on what every participant believed or experienced.

### Britannica overview
- Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Milgram experiment,” updated Apr. 25, 2026.
- URL: https://www.britannica.com/science/Milgram-experiment
- Key use: accessible fact-check for Yale setting, early-1960s timing, New Haven recruitment, $4.50 payment, 15–450V shock board, staged learner reactions, ethical criticism, and broad summary of disputed legacy.
- Caveat: secondary overview; cite primary papers for central numbers.

### Burger partial replication / modern ethical limit
- Jerry M. Burger, “Replicating Milgram: Would People Still Obey Today?”, *American Psychologist*, 64(1), 1–11, 2009.
- Related PubMed commentary: Arthur G. Miller, “Reflections on ‘Replicating Milgram’ (Burger, 2009),” *American Psychologist*, 64(1), 20–27, 2009. URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19209960/
- Key use: modern partial replication stopped at 150 volts; ethically modified design; high continuation rate comparable enough to raise the same problem without reproducing the original harm.
- Caveat: not a full 450V replication. Avoid saying it “proved the same 65% today.” It showed many participants would continue past the learner's first major protest under modified conditions.

### Haslam and Reicher reinterpretation
- S. Alexander Haslam and Stephen D. Reicher, “Contesting the ‘Nature’ Of Conformity: What Milgram and Zimbardo's Studies Really Show,” *PLOS Biology*, 10(11): e1001426, 2012. URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1001426
- Key use: challenges passive-conformity mythology; proposes that people follow when they identify with an authority and believe the cause is right — “engaged followership.”
- Caveat: this is interpretation and theory, not a replacement for Milgram's data.

### Archival criticism / participant belief
- Gina Perry, *Behind the Shock Machine: The Untold Story of the Notorious Milgram Psychology Experiments*, The New Press, 2013.
- Related discussion via APA teacher resources and reviews.
- Key use: later archival/interview work raised problems: inconsistent procedures, participant skepticism, more coercive deviations from scripted prods in some sessions, and questions about debriefing and long-term participant impact.
- Caveat: present as criticism and complication, not as total debunking. The honest line: the 65% result came from one famous condition; the broader archive is messier than the textbook myth.

### Milgram's broader book
- Stanley Milgram, *Obedience to Authority: An Experimental View*, Harper & Row, 1974.
- Key use: Milgram's later synthesis, including multiple variations and “agentic state” framing.
- Caveat: same authorial limitations as the original study; useful for Milgram's own theory.

## Ethical/context notes
- Do not imply anyone was actually electrocuted. The learner was not shocked; the participant's distress came from believing harm might be real.
- Do not frame participants as monsters. The stronger and more accurate frame is ordinary people under institutional pressure, experiencing conflict.
- Do not use Holocaust explanation loosely. Milgram was influenced by Eichmann and postwar questions about obedience, but the Holocaust cannot be reduced to a laboratory effect.
- Do not say “science proves we all obey.” The evidence is conditional: setting, authority, legitimacy, distance from victim, institutional framing, and belief all matter.
- Keep the horror grounded in procedure: a chair, a switchboard, a man in a lab coat, a voice from another room, and the sentence “the experiment requires that you continue.”
