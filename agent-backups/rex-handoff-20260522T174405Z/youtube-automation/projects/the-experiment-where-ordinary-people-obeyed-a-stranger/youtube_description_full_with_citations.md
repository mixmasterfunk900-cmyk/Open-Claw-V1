# YouTube Description — The Experiment Where Ordinary People Obeyed A Stranger

## Title
The Experiment Where Ordinary People Obeyed A Stranger

## Description
In 1961, Stanley Milgram brought ordinary men into a Yale laboratory, sat them in front of a shock machine labeled from 15 volts to 450 volts, and told them they were taking part in a study of memory and learning.

The “learner” in the other room begged them to stop.

The man in the lab coat said continue.

The shocks were fake. The obedience was not.

In Milgram’s first published condition, 26 out of 40 participants — 65% — went all the way to the maximum 450-volt switch. Many were not calm while doing it. They sweated, trembled, argued, laughed nervously, asked who would be responsible, and still kept pressing the next switch.

This video looks at what the Milgram obedience experiment actually showed — and what the simplified version gets wrong.

It was not simply proof that “people blindly obey authority.” The darker lesson is more conditional and more useful: harm becomes easier when it is gradual, distant, framed as duty, backed by a respected institution, and split into steps so small that no single step feels like becoming cruel.

We also look at the modern debate around Milgram’s work: the ethical problems, participant distress, deception, questions about how much some participants believed, Jerry Burger’s 2009 partial replication, and Haslam and Reicher’s argument that obedience often works through “engaged followership” — people identifying with the mission, not just submitting to a person.

The machine is history now.

The shape of the room is not.

## Notes on accuracy
The learner in Milgram’s experiment was not actually shocked. The harm came from participants being deceived into believing the shocks might be real. The video treats the famous 65% result as Milgram’s published baseline condition, while also acknowledging later criticism that the full archive was messier than the simple textbook version.
