# Source notes — The Experiment Where Children Were Taught to Fear

## Historical experiment

- John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner conducted the Little Albert experiment at Johns Hopkins University in 1920.
- Subject was an infant referred to as “Albert B.”, commonly described as around 11 months old during the conditioning sessions.
- Method: Albert was first shown neutral stimuli including a white rat. Then the white rat was paired with a loud noise made by striking a steel bar behind him.
- Result: Albert began to cry and withdraw from the rat. His fear generalized to similar furry/white objects such as a rabbit, dog, fur coat, cotton wool, and a Santa Claus mask.
- Ethical limitation: Albert was not deconditioned before leaving the hospital.
- Identity dispute: Hall P. Beck and colleagues argued in 2009 that Albert was Douglas Merritte. Later work disputed this and proposed William Barger. Treat identity as unresolved.

## Modern parallel 1 — observational fear learning

- Susan Mineka, Michael Cook, and colleagues studied observational fear learning in rhesus monkeys in the 1980s at the University of Wisconsin.
- Key point: monkeys could acquire fear responses by observing another monkey react fearfully to snakes. The work helped show fear can be socially transmitted, not only learned through direct injury.

## Modern parallel 2 — neural mechanism of fear learning

- Joseph LeDoux’s work at New York University in the 1990s helped establish the amygdala as central to fear conditioning and threat learning.
- Elizabeth Phelps and colleagues at New York University studied human fear learning and extinction, connecting conditioned fear to amygdala activity and later regulation.

## Edge case — unlearning fear

- Mary Cover Jones, working in the 1920s, used counterconditioning with a young child known as Peter. A feared rabbit was gradually brought closer while Peter ate pleasant food. This became an early example of behavior therapy.

## Script cautions

- Do not overclaim that Albert’s full adult life is known; identity and later outcome remain disputed.
- Do not moralize; present the facts clinically.
- State the result in the opening 45 seconds.
- Use exact names, years, and institutions where possible.
